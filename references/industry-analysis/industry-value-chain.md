# Industry Value Chain

## Core Concept
Map the full chain from raw materials to end users, identifying value creation and profit distribution at each stage. Reveals profit pools, shift trends, and strategic positioning opportunities.

## Applicable Scenarios
✅ **Best for**
- Industry entry decisions
- Value chain positioning
- M&A target screening

⚠️ **When NOT to use**
- Digital products with no physical chain — raw materials to retail decomposes into nothing, and the real constraints are channel and network effects → **Wardley Mapping**
- You compete inside one stage, not across the chain — mapping the chain tells you nothing about your own unit economics or segment attractiveness → segment-level pricing and margin analysis instead
- The chain is stable and the question is growth mechanics — value-chain mapping is a positioning tool, not an execution plan → **North Star Framework** and **Cohort Analysis**
- Stage data is thin or hearsay — profit pools claimed from memory or a single vendor report; a chain built on invented numbers produces confident nonsense → source each stage's economics before drawing conclusions

## Key Steps
1. Map the industry value chain stages (raw materials → manufacturing → distribution → retail → end user)
2. For each stage, estimate: revenue pool, profit margin, growth rate
3. Identify profit pool shifts: where are margins migrating?
4. Assess your current position and potential entry points
5. Evaluate competitive dynamics at each stage
6. Make positioning decision based on profit pool trends

## Output Template

```
Chain definition: [stages from [upstream source] to end user, named for this industry]
Stage economics: [stage | revenue pool | margin % | growth % | concentration | data source]
Profit pool shift: [from stage → to stage | direction | time window | driver]
Our position: [stage we compete in | what we own vs. what we outsource]
Entry candidates: [stage | why it is attractive | what we would need | main barrier]
Chosen positioning: [stage + what we do differently | why it is defensible]
```

## Failure Modes
- Generic chain: the same raw-materials-to-retail template pasted onto every industry → draw the chain from this industry's actual transactions; a generic chain produces generic strategy
- Unsupported numbers: profit pools estimated from memory and presented as fact → tag every estimate with its source and confidence, and mark unknowns as unknown rather than interpolating them
- Chain as a snapshot: margins move faster than the map → re-derive the pool split on a fixed cadence and note which single assumption the conclusion depends on
- Entry chosen by the map alone: an attractive pool is assumed to be enterable → test entry feasibility (capital, channel access, incumbent response) before committing

## Evidence Strength
Practitioner consensus — Porter's value chain is a durable structuring tool with a large strategy-practice and consulting base, and the underlying mechanics (value accrues where differentiation and buyer power are favorable) are well established. The mapping itself is an interpretive act rather than a measurement, and stage-level profit data is frequently thin, proprietary, or estimated, so the framework reliably generates hypotheses and entry candidates while its conclusions inherit the quality of the numbers fed into it.

## Source
Michael Porter, *Competitive Advantage* (1985); value chain analysis framework.
