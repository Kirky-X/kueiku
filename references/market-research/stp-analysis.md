# STP Analysis

## Core Concept
3-step marketing strategy framework: Segmentation (divide market into segments) → Targeting (select which segments to serve) → Positioning (define how to be perceived in target segments).

## Applicable Scenarios
✅ **Best for**
- Market entry strategy
- Product positioning
- Marketing strategy development

⚠️ **When NOT to use**
- Single-segment products born from one community — there's nothing to divide; go straight to positioning work
- Segment variables chosen because data exists, not because they predict buying behavior — demographics that don't correlate with need are noise
- As strategy when the real problem is distribution or product-market fit — STP organizes demand you've already proven

## Key Steps
1. **Segmentation**: Divide the market using demographic, behavioral, or needs-based variables
2. **Targeting**: Evaluate each segment (size, growth, competition, fit); select target segments
3. **Positioning**: Define your positioning in target segments (see Positioning Strategy)
4. Develop marketing mix (4Ps) aligned to STP decisions
5. Validate with market data

## Output Template

```
Market: [definition]

Segmentation basis: [needs-based preferred — variable + why it predicts buying]
  | Segment     | Size | Growth | Competition | Fit with our assets | Verdict |
  | [SMB admins]| $[x] | [g%]   | [n players] | [high/med/low]      | target  |
  | [segment 2] | ...  | ...    | ...         | ...                 | skip — [reason] |

Target(s): [1-2 max — more targets means no positioning]
Positioning statement: For [target] who [need], [product] is the [category] that [key benefit], unlike [main alternative].
Downstream: 4Ps aligned [product/price/place/promotion one line each]
Validation: [segment-size source; message test result]
```

## Failure Modes
- Segmentation by census: dividing by age/income brackets that don't move purchase behavior → prefer needs/behavioral segmentation; a segment exists only if it responds differently
- Target-everything: three "primary" segments, three watered-down positionings → targeting is a decision; if nothing was rejected, revisit step 2
- Positioning disconnected from proof: claims the product can't substantiate → each positioning claim needs the evidence trail (reviews, win/loss, data)

## Evidence Strength
Practitioner consensus — the backbone of marketing strategy teaching since Kotler; conceptually sound and universally used, but segment evaluation inputs (size, growth) are frequently estimates, and there's little controlled evidence on framework use translating to outcomes — quality is determined by the market data feeding it.

## Source
Philip Kotler, *Marketing Management*; foundational marketing framework.
