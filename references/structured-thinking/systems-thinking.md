# Systems Thinking

## Core Concept
Understand complex systems through feedback loops (reinforcing and balancing), causal loop diagrams, and leverage point analysis. Reveals why solutions that work short-term often worsen problems long-term.

## Applicable Scenarios
✅ **Best for**
- Recurring problems
- Solutions that work short-term but worsen long-term
- Complex system analysis

## Key Steps
1. Define the system boundary and key variables
2. Map causal relationships between variables (causal loop diagram)
3. Identify feedback loops: reinforcing (amplifying) and balancing (stabilizing)
4. Match the loop structure to a system archetype (table below); if none fits, say so — don't force one
5. Find leverage points on Meadows' hierarchy (below): intuition digs at parameters; durable change lives higher
6. Mark each candidate's **wrong-direction risk**: intuitive push vs. correct push (Meadows' warning — people find the right knob and turn it the wrong way; on reinforcing-loop gain, intuition amplifies, leverage damps)
7. Test interventions mentally for second-order effects; design at the chosen leverage point and monitor for unintended consequences

## System Archetypes

| Archetype | Signature | Breakthrough direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Limits to Growth | Growth hits a delayed constraint | Relieve the constraint; don't push growth harder |
| Shifting the Burden | Quick fix erodes the fundamental capability | Fund the fundamental fix; tolerate the symptom |
| Fixes That Fail | The fix feeds a loop that worsens the problem | Slow down; name the unintended loop |
| Drifting Goals | The standard erodes to close the gap | Anchor the goal to an external absolute |
| Escalation | Two parties act on a relative measure | Drop the comparative measure; de-escalate unilaterally |
| Growth & Underinvestment | Capacity trails demand; standards track past performance | Invest ahead of demand |
| Success to the Successful | Allocation bets on last cycle's winner | Break the winner-takes-all allocation |
| Tragedy of the Commons | Individual gain from a shared resource | Make the collective loss visible and immediate |

## Leverage Hierarchy (Meadows)

Weakest → strongest: 12 parameters · 11 buffers · 10 stock-flow structure · 9 delays · 8 balancing-loop strength · 7 reinforcing-loop gain · 6 information flows · 5 rules/incentives · 4 self-organization · 3 goals · 2 paradigms · 1 transcending paradigms.

## When NOT to use

- The problem is not a system — a single broken component with a known fix needs a repair, not a loop diagram
- Data is too thin to map causal relationships — a loop drawn on one anecdote is a picture of an assumption, and the archetype match will be confabulated
- Time pressure demands a decision this month — mapping loops and locating leverage is slow by design; say so instead of shipping a shallow diagram
- The system boundary cannot be agreed on — every party drawing a different boundary produces two diagrams and a new argument
- Single-loop mechanics with known parameters — tuning the parameter is faster and safer than restructuring the loop

## Output Template

```
Boundary: [what is inside / explicitly outside]
Variables: [2–6, with the one that actually drives behavior marked]
Reinforcing loop: [R: A↑ → B↑ → A↑] | Balancing loop: [B: A↑ → B↑ → A↓]
Archetype: [name from the table, or "no archetype fits — structure is novel"]
Leverage point: [Meadows level, weakest → strongest]
Wrong-direction risk: [what intuition does here, and what the correct push is]
Second-order watch: [what gets worse downstream, and the monitoring signal]
```

## Failure Modes

- **Forced archetype match** — the table has eight patterns; resembling two of them does not make a system one of them; "no archetype fits" is a valid and often correct output
- **Diagram without data** — arrows drawn from plausibility read as findings; mark inferred links as inferred
- **Leverage claimed at the parameter level** — "increase X by 10%" presented as a structural fix; on reinforcing loops intuition pushing harder is the documented failure mode, not the exception
- **Boundary drawn to get the answer you want** — excluding an inconvenient variable makes the loop look manageable; state what was left out and why

## Evidence Strength

**Strong for structure, weaker for prediction.** Meadows' leverage hierarchy and the archetype catalog come from decades of systems practice and are widely replicated; Senge's archetype work rests on a case-study base. Predictive power is genuinely contested: correctly mapping a loop structure does not reliably predict an intervention's outcome — which is why the wrong-direction check and second-order watch above are mandatory rather than optional.
