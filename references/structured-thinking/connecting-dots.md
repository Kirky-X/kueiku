# Connecting Dots

## Core Concept
Cross-domain knowledge connection sparks innovation. By deliberately combining insights from different fields, novel solutions emerge that single-domain thinking cannot produce.

## Applicable Scenarios
✅ **Best for**
- Innovation stimulation
- Cross-disciplinary thinking
- Personal growth planning

⚠️ **When NOT to use**
- The problem is well-structured and the domain has a standard method — forced cross-domain analogy adds noise; use **First Principles** or the domain's own tools
- You have no dots: no reading, no adjacent-domain exposure, no collected mechanisms — the method recombines material, it cannot conjure it; gather first
- Execution-critical moments on a known-good path — novelty is a cost here, not a feature
- As a substitute for depth — a metaphor borrowed from another field is not evidence; validate the borrowed mechanism in this domain before acting on it

## Key Steps
1. Identify the problem or opportunity
2. Gather dots: collect insights, experiences, and knowledge from diverse domains
3. Look for patterns: what connections exist between seemingly unrelated dots?
4. Force connections: "How would [domain X] approach this problem?"
5. Evaluate the novel connections for feasibility and value
6. Develop the most promising connections into actionable ideas

## Output Template

```
Problem: [the question worth connecting dots for]
Dots inventory:
  - [domain A]: [insight / mechanism that worked there]
  - [domain B]: [insight / mechanism]
Forced connections: "How would [domain X] approach this?" → [candidate idea]
Pattern check: [what the candidates share / why the transfer might hold]
Feasibility + value screen: [idea → viable? valuable? / reason discarded]
Developed idea: [the one connection worth prototyping + its first cheap test]
```

## Failure Modes
- Metaphor mistaken for mechanism: "we'll run like Spotify squads" transfers the vocabulary, not the operating conditions → name the causal mechanism being borrowed and check whether this domain satisfies its preconditions
- Dot scarcity dressed as insight: recombining two shallow exposures yields shallow output → depth in at least one domain is the entry fee; go gather more dots instead
- Novelty bias: the surprising connection wins attention over the useful one → screen candidates on feasibility and value, never on surprise
- Connection without validation: the idea is announced, never tested → convert the best candidate into a cheap experiment with a falsifiable expectation

## Evidence Strength
Practitioner consensus, weakly evidenced — combinatorial creativity and analogical transfer are documented in cognitive science and many recorded breakthroughs are cross-domain recombinations, but "deliberately cross-pollinate to innovate" is a describing pattern, not a validated procedure: no studies show forced-connection exercises outperform deep single-domain work at equal time investment, and most borrowed analogies fail their preconditions check.

## Source
Steve Jobs Stanford commencement speech (2005): "You can't connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards."
