# B2B Buying Center Mapping

## Core Concept
In committee purchases, no single person buys — and deal stalls are **role problems before they are product problems**. Map the buying center roles as they exist in this specific account: **initiator, gatekeeper, influencer, decider, buyer, user** (Webster & Wind's organizational buying model). Each role is measured on something different and loses something different, so each needs a different answer: the champion's business case does not answer procurement's risk mandate. The map's output is per-role coverage: who has been spoken to, and who has not.

## Applicable Scenarios
✅ **Best for**
- Stalled or slow B2B deals — locating whose objection is actually blocking
- Account planning before an ABM motion (GTM Motions disqualifies ABM "without account data" — this is that data)
- Enterprise or multi-stakeholder purchases of any kind

⚠️ **When NOT to use**
- Single-buyer consumer products → **Consumer Decision Journey** models the individual loop
- Deciding WHICH accounts to pursue → **Ideal Customer Profile** (firm-level); this entry maps WHO within the account
- Internal organizational influence and change → **Stakeholder Mapping** (Strategic Analysis) covers your own org and policy stakeholders, not the customer's buying group
- Writing per-role content and copy → owned messaging territory (**GTM Strategy** frame); this entry produces the role table the content must answer

## Key Steps
1. List every person who touches the purchase in this account — including the ones you have not met (the invisible gatekeeper is the standard finding)
2. Assign roles: initiator (felt the problem), gatekeeper (controls access), influencer (shapes the spec), decider (owns the yes), buyer (owns the budget signature), user (lives with the choice)
3. For each role, write what they are measured on and what losing looks like for them — the decider's loss frame is rarely the champion's
4. Identify the true decider and the true gatekeeper; champions are frequently neither
5. Map current coverage: which roles has anyone on your side actually spoken to; uncovered roles are where deals go quiet
6. Align the motion per role: champion gets the business case, gatekeeper gets the risk and compliance answer, buyer gets the procurement path, user gets the workflow demo, influencer gets the spec detail
7. Re-map at every deal-stage change — reorgs and stage transitions invalidate role maps silently

## Output Template

```
Account: [name] — deal stage [..]
| Role | Person | Measured on | What losing looks like | Covered? | Next action |
Champion vs decider: [same person? if not — who actually signs]
Gatekeeper: [who controls access + their filter criterion]
Coverage gaps: [roles with zero contact]
Per-role motion: [champion → business case | gatekeeper → risk answer | buyer → procurement path | user → demo]
Stall diagnosis: [the blocking objection → whose role it belongs to]
Re-map trigger: [stage change | reorg | new stakeholder appears]
```

## Failure Modes
- Champion mistaken for decider: the enthusiast signs nothing → separate influence from authority explicitly; ask "who signs" and "who can veto" as two different questions
- Procurement treated as an obstacle: it is a role with a risk mandate → give it the answer it is measured on (compliance, exit terms, references), not more enthusiasm
- Single-threaded deal: one contact means one reorg away from zero → coverage gaps are the actionable output; close them before the stage advances
- Stale role map: a stage change or reorg silently invalidates it → re-map on every stage transition
- The user ignored: the person who lives with the deployment can veto later or starve adoption → users are mapped and covered, not skipped as "not decision-makers"
- Uniform pitch to every role: the business case answers a question procurement is not asking → per-role loss frames drive per-role motions

## Evidence Strength
**Practitioner consensus, descriptive taxonomy.** The organizational buying model (Webster & Wind 1968; role set canonically presented in their 1972 book) has anchored B2B sales practice for five decades and the role vocabulary is stable across sales methodologies; validation sits mostly in sales-practice literature rather than controlled studies, and modern revisions add roles (coach, economic buyer) without displacing the core six. Use it as a coverage checklist, not a predictive model of who will do what.
